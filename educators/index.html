How to ensure academic integrity in the age of AI

Last updated on 17 May 2026

AI can speed up work for students, but as a teacher you still want to protect real understanding, independent thinking, and accountable academic work.

This guide offers a practical starting point: teaching students how AI works is important, but it is not enough on its own. Students also need opportunities to discuss real situations and decide together what appropriate AI use means in your course.

This guide includes short definitions, realistic teaching situations, and practical steps you can apply right away.

Why judgment needs practice

Knowing AI rules and understanding how AI works are important, but they do not automatically lead to good decisions in real assignments. Research on integrity training shows that rules mainly build knowledge, while discussion of concrete, personal cases strengthens judgment in practice.
In other words, to help students decide what appropriate AI use is, engage them in dialogue about real-life situations, surface value tensions, and work through possible responses together.

Three integrity definitions

These three definitions can help you shape both your course policy and your day-to-day communication with students.

Academic integrity

Academic integrity concerns student conduct in coursework. It is commonly framed through values such as honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility, and courage.

ICAI academic integrity reference

Research integrity

Research integrity concerns conduct in scientific practice: how evidence is produced, how findings are reported, and how collaborators are credited with honesty, care, transparency, independence, and responsibility.

Nederlandse gedragscode wetenschappelijke integriteit (2018)

AI integrity is not a separate value system. It is the capacity to use generative AI in ways that are consistent with these existing integrity values — especially when those values come into tension in real coursework decisions.

How values and integrity align: Values are ideal beliefs about what matters (for example, honesty, fairness, or transparency). Integrity in practice means making choices that stay aligned with those values, especially when values pull in different directions.

From an Aristotelian view, integrity is developed through practice and habit, not only by knowing rules. This is why case discussion matters: students learn to find a balanced position rather than an extreme. For example, transparency can be too little (hiding AI use) or too much (overloading a paper with process detail so it becomes unreadable). The integrity task is to find the responsible middle for the context.

Situations that may feel familiar

These are realistic situations many teachers encounter, described in a personal and practical way.

Use these as personal cases, not abstract examples: ask students what they would do, why, and what they might lose or gain with that choice.

"The student disclosed AI use, but the draft still feels off"

Situation: "A student submits a formative draft and clearly discloses AI use. While reading, you notice that one key term is factually incorrect in this context and does not match the course content or assigned readings. The writing looks fluent, but there is a clear mistake and you are unsure whether the student understands the idea behind the wording."

Guiding questions:

1. Which integrity values are at play in this situation?

2. Which values are in conflict here, and how does that tension play out in practice?

Hint
The student was transparent about AI use, but the factual error raises questions about care (did the student verify the output?) and responsibility (who is accountable for the mistake?). How do those pull in opposite directions?

3. What would you do?

Hint
Think about what the teacher should do next: address the error privately with the student, raise it as a learning moment for the class, or something else? And what should the student do differently when submitting AI-assisted work?

"A student asks for a non-AI route"

Situation: "You designed an assignment where AI supports the learning process. A student asks to opt out because using AI conflicts with their environmental values. The student is motivated and wants to meet the same learning goals, but does not want to use the tool itself."

Guiding questions:

1. Which integrity values are at play in this situation?

2. Which values are in conflict here, and how does that tension play out in practice?

Hint
Respecting the student's personal values pulls toward autonomy and respect, but allowing an individual exception raises questions about fairness to the rest of the class. How do those pull in opposite directions?

3. What would you do?

Hint
Think about what the teacher should decide: grant the exception, deny it, or find a middle path — and how to explain that decision to the student and to the class as a whole.

"I restrict AI because I cannot supervise it well yet"

Situation: "In your course, AI is currently restricted because it is not required for the intended outcomes and you do not yet feel confident supervising its use responsibly. Some students challenge this and argue that AI is already standard in academic and professional practice."

Guiding questions:

1. Which integrity values are at play in this situation?

2. Which values are in conflict here, and how does that tension play out in practice?

Hint
The teacher's caution reflects care and responsibility in supervision, but students point to a transparency gap: AI is already standard in practice, so is restricting it honest about what students will face? How do those pull in opposite directions?

3. What would you do?

Hint
Think about what the teacher should do: maintain the restriction, explain the reasoning more explicitly, or begin preparing to lift it. And what should students do when they disagree with a course policy?

"The student relies on AI for every step"

Situation: "During supervision, a student explains their workflow: AI generated the topic options, research questions, outline, first draft, and revision suggestions. The final text is coherent, but when you ask why key choices were made, the student struggles to explain them without reopening the tool."

Guiding questions:

1. Which integrity values are at play in this situation?

2. Which values are in conflict here, and how does that tension play out in practice?

Hint
The student completed the task and the output is coherent, which looks responsible — but independence in reasoning seems to be absent: every key decision was delegated to AI. How do responsibility and independence pull in opposite directions?

3. What would you do?

Hint
Think about what the teacher should do: intervene in this supervision meeting, adjust the assignment design for future students, or both. And what should the student do differently to show that the reasoning is genuinely their own?

"The argument looks strong, but the evaluation is missing"

Situation: "A student submits a polished analysis with confident claims and smooth transitions. On closer reading, the text repeats AI-generated points but does not evaluate evidence quality, compare sources, or question assumptions. The argument sounds convincing, yet critical thinking is largely absent."

Guiding questions:

1. Which integrity values are at play in this situation?

2. Which values are in conflict here, and how does that tension play out in practice?

Hint
The output is fluent and confident, which may look like reliable, careful work — but care and reliability require evaluating evidence and questioning assumptions, which is absent here. How do surface quality and genuine critical thinking pull in opposite directions?

3. What would you do?

Hint
Think about what the teacher should do: give feedback on the current submission, redesign the assessment prompt to require visible critical thinking, or both. And what should the student do differently to show the analysis is genuinely their own?

The values behind your policy

These five values are the ones most directly under pressure in AI-related coursework dilemmas.

Independence

Students show their own reasoning and judgment.

Honesty

Students are truthful about authorship and assistance.

Transparency

Students clearly disclose where AI contributed.

Care

Students verify, check, and revise outputs critically.

Responsibility

Students remain accountable for final decisions and claims.

Fairness

Expectations and assessment are applied consistently and impartially across students.

Respect

Students and teachers acknowledge intellectual contributions and engage with differing views responsibly.

Trust

Learning depends on confidence that claims, disclosures, and feedback are reliable and sincere.

Courage

Students act in line with integrity values even when doing so is difficult or risky.

Reliability

Work is methodologically sound, with careful use of evidence, tools, and reasoning steps.

Accountability

Learners can explain and justify process choices, outcomes, and consequences of AI-supported decisions.

Good Stewardship

Students handle sources, data, and shared resources responsibly on behalf of the academic community.

Three practical steps you can use now

Use these steps in order: first make AI use visible, then explain your reason, then discuss a real case together.

Tip 1: Make disclosure concrete

Ask students to describe the tool, the task, and what they changed after using AI output.

This helps clarity, but it does not by itself prove good judgment.

Use: the disclosure guide to make expectations specific and workable.

Open the disclosure builder

Tip 2: Explain your rationale, not only your rule

Students comply more consistently when they understand the learning purpose behind policy choices.

Go beyond "allowed/not allowed": explain what learning goal you are protecting and why.

Use: the rationale page in your syllabus, Canvas, or class discussion.

Open the rationale builder

Tip 3: Choose a case or use your own

Start with a case from the collection, or bring a case from your own teaching where values come into tension.

Use a real classroom case. Ask learners to choose an action, explain it, and think through what could happen next.

Use: compare possible responses, then contribute your own case so colleagues can learn across contexts.
